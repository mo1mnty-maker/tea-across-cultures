01 · Introduction

One plant, different tea traditions

Tea is enjoyed across Asia, but it does not look or taste the same everywhere. As it moved between cultures, people adapted it to their beliefs, routines, and local preferences.

This project compares Japan in East Asia and Thailand in Southeast Asia. Transculturation describes tea crossing from one cultural area to another. Diffusion and transformation describes how tea practices changed after arriving. China is important to both stories, but the traditions that developed in Japan and Thailand are not identical.

02 · Japan

From China to Japan: the arrival of tea

Tea reached Japan through contact with China. Japanese monks and diplomatic travelers who visited China brought tea-drinking practices back with them. An early record describes the monk Eichū serving tea to Emperor Saga in 815. At first, tea was mainly associated with Buddhist temples and the upper levels of society.

Tea became more established during the Kamakura period. The Buddhist monk Eisai returned from China in 1191 with tea seeds and knowledge of powdered tea. Tea was valued by monks because it helped them stay alert during meditation, and it later became popular among samurai and wealthy households (Japan Tourism Agency, 2020).

03 · Japan

How Japanese tea traditions changed

After tea arrived, it gradually became more than a drink. In Japan it developed into chanoyu, the Japanese tea ceremony. The ceremony places importance on hospitality, respect, careful preparation, and attention to the moment. Matcha, a powdered green tea whisked with hot water, became closely connected to this tradition.

Tea also developed beyond formal ceremonies. Sencha is made by steeping tea leaves in hot water, rather than whisking powdered tea. The spread of different tea styles gave people more ways to enjoy tea in everyday life. Japan's tea culture changed through diffusion: a practice introduced from China was adapted into local customs, rituals, and methods of preparation.

Matcha

Powdered green tea whisked with hot water. It is closely associated with chanoyu and formal hospitality.

Sencha

Loose tea leaves steeped in hot water. It became another important way to enjoy Japanese green tea.

Sources: Japan Tourism Agency (2020); Kyoto National Museum (2022).

04 · Thailand

Tea in Thailand: trade, migration, and local history

Thailand's tea history developed through several cultural connections. Chinese merchants and migrant communities helped bring Chinese tea-drinking customs into Thai society. Trade and migration allowed these practices to become part of social life, especially in communities with strong connections to China.

Thailand's tea history is not only about imported drinking customs. Northern Thailand has a long tradition of miang, fermented tea leaves that are eaten or chewed. Research on miang describes its ethnobotanical history and its place in northern Thai culture (Khanongnuch et al., 2017). These traditions show that tea use in Thailand developed in more than one way.

05 · Thailand

How Thai tea traditions changed

One well-known example of Thai tea culture is cha yen, or Thai iced tea. It is commonly served cold and sweetened, often with milk. Its flavor and appearance are different from the hot, unsweetened tea commonly associated with many Chinese tea traditions. Local preferences and the popularity of chilled sweet drinks helped make it a recognizable part of modern Thai food culture.

Another example is miang. The photographs above show fermented tea leaves prepared in compact portions and wrapped in leaves. Rather than being prepared as a sweet drink, miang uses fermented tea leaves that are eaten or chewed. Fermentation changes the leaves and their properties. Research has examined the nutritional changes that occur during traditional miang fermentation (Unban et al., 2019).

These examples show that tea in Thailand was shaped by different histories and local preferences. Trade and migration helped bring tea-drinking customs across borders, while northern Thai practices and modern tastes shaped how tea was used after it arrived.

Two different forms of transformation: Cha yen shows how tea became a sweet, chilled drink. Miang shows how tea leaves became a fermented food tradition.
06 · Comparative analysis

Two countries, different tea traditions

Japan

  • Tea brought through monks and travelers from China.
  • Buddhist practice influenced early use.
  • Matcha became central to chanoyu.
  • Sencha expanded everyday tea choices.

Thailand

  • Chinese trade and migration helped spread tea customs.
  • Northern communities maintained miang traditions.
  • Cha yen became a popular sweet, chilled drink.
  • Tea is used as both a drink and a fermented food.

Japan and Thailand were both influenced by China, but their tea traditions developed differently because tea entered different social settings and met different local customs. In Japan, Buddhist connections helped give tea a role in religious life and later in the tea ceremony. The development of matcha and sencha shows how tea practices were adapted for both formal gatherings and everyday use.

In Thailand, Chinese migration and trade helped introduce tea-drinking customs, while northern Thai communities had their own miang tradition. Later, sweetened iced tea became another recognizable form. This comparison shows that transculturation describes tea crossing cultural borders, while diffusion and transformation describe how people reshape it after it becomes part of their culture. Religion, migration, local food customs, and everyday preferences all influenced the results.
